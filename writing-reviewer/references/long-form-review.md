# Reviewing Theses, Dissertations, Papers and Long Reports

Long documents fail differently from essays. The common problems are not in any one sentence but in the relationships between parts: a research question that drifts, a method that can't answer it, results the discussion ignores, terms that change meaning between chapters. Review with that in mind.

## 1. Map the whole before judging any part

Before detailed reading, skim the full document and write a **document map**:

- Title, research question(s) / aims / hypotheses — quoted exactly as written, and noting every place they're stated (abstract, intro, methods, conclusion). Mismatches between these statements are one of the most common and most costly problems.
- Chapter/section list with word count and one-line purpose each.
- The claimed contribution (in the writer's words).

Check **proportion**: does the word count per chapter match its importance? (A 12,000-word dissertation with a 5,000-word literature review and a 1,000-word discussion has a balance problem.)

## 2. Keep a consistency ledger while reading

As you go, record in a running list:

- **Key terms and definitions** — flag a term used in two senses, or defined after first use.
- **Abbreviations** — defined at first use? used consistently? defined twice?
- **Numbers** — sample sizes, percentages, dates, statistics that appear more than once. Check they agree across abstract, results, tables and discussion.
- **Claims promised** ("as Chapter 5 will show…") — check each promise is kept.
- **Figures/tables** — numbered in order, each referred to in the text, captions self-explanatory, axes/units labelled.
- **Citations** — in-text vs. reference list; style consistency; suspicious or unverifiable references (mark "verify").
- **Tense, person, and spelling variety** (UK vs. US) consistency.

Report ledger findings in the "Consistency" part of the review; they are cheap to fix and examiners notice them.

## 3. Section-by-section checklists

**Abstract** — Does it state the problem, method, key findings (with numbers where relevant) and implication, within the limit? Could someone decide whether to read on from it alone? Does it match what the document actually found?

**Introduction** — Context → problem → gap → question/aim → approach → contribution → roadmap. Is the gap real and specifically evidenced, not just asserted ("little research has been done")? Is the question answerable within the scope?

**Literature review** — Synthesis by theme or debate, not a list of summaries. Critical evaluation of studies (design, sample, validity). Does it build towards the gap and justify the chosen approach? Are seminal and recent works both present? Is the theoretical framework explained and then actually *used* later?

**Methodology** — Is the design appropriate for the question (and is that justified against alternatives)? Sampling, instruments, procedure, analysis — replicable in detail? Validity, reliability (quant) or trustworthiness, reflexivity (qual) addressed? Ethics approval and consent where relevant? Limitations acknowledged honestly? Does the analysis plan match the data actually collected?

**Results / findings** — Reported without interpretation (in disciplines that separate them). Statistics reported fully (test, statistic, df, p, effect size, CI as the discipline expects); no "p = 0.000"; no claiming significance for non-significant results or "trends". Qualitative themes evidenced with quotes, and not just a list of topics. Tables and figures not duplicating each other or the text.

**Discussion** — Each finding interpreted and related back to the research question and the literature. Unexpected findings explained, not ignored. Causal language only where the design supports it. Limitations specific, with their likely effect on the findings. Implications specific (for whom, what to do). No new results introduced.

**Conclusion** — Directly answers each research question. States the contribution clearly. Future research specific, not "more research is needed".

**Front and back matter** — Table of contents matches headings; list of figures/tables; appendices referred to in text; declaration/acknowledgements per institution requirements.

## 4. The golden thread test

At the end, trace one line: **question → literature gap → method → findings → answer → contribution**. If any link is weak or missing, that's almost always the most important issue in the review, and it belongs at the top of "What would move this up a band".

## 5. Working through very long documents

For documents over ~15,000 words (or when the text is too long to hold in view reliably):

1. Build the document map (§1) first.
2. Review one chapter at a time, completing the ledger as you go, and producing per-chapter notes: strengths, 🔴/🟠 issues, line corrections.
3. Then write the overall review from the chapter notes plus the golden-thread test, giving one overall mark and optional per-chapter scores.
4. Put line-level corrections in chapter order; if they're numerous, give the most important in the review and offer the full list (or a tracked-changes document) as a separate deliverable.

Never skim the middle chapters to save effort; say explicitly if any part was not reviewed and why.

## 6. Examiner-style questions

For theses and dissertations, add a short section **"Questions an examiner would ask"** — 3 to 6 pointed questions the writer should be ready to answer in a viva or defence (e.g., "Why did you choose thematic analysis over IPA given your research question about lived experience?"). This is highly valuable for thesis writers and forces the review to engage with the substance.

## 7. Stage-appropriate feedback

- **Early draft / proposal:** focus almost entirely on question, scope, design and structure. Don't line-edit prose that may be cut.
- **Full draft:** everything, weighted towards the golden thread and discussion.
- **Final / pre-submission:** everything, with extra attention to consistency ledger, referencing, formatting requirements and the abstract.

If you don't know the stage, infer from completeness and polish, and say which you assumed.
